## Lab: Forecasting Architecture and the Bias-Variance Tradeoff

**Domain:** Predictive Modelling · Statistical Learning Theory · Model Risk Management  
**Tools:** Python, Pandas, NumPy, Scikit-Learn (`PolynomialFeatures`, `LinearRegression`, `cross_val_score`), Matplotlib  
**Dataset:** NVIDIA Quarterly Financials — Total Corporate Revenue, 2024–2026

---

### Objective

To expose the catastrophic out-of-sample failure mode of a high-variance polynomial model on NVIDIA financial data — demonstrating through K-Fold Cross-Validation that near-zero training error is not a signal of model quality but a diagnostic of overfitting, and that unregularised complexity produces hallucinated forecasts whose error magnitude constitutes a quantifiable operational risk.

---

### Methodology

- **Polynomial Feature Expansion (Degree 7):** Transformed the quarterly time index into a 7th-degree polynomial feature set using Scikit-Learn's `PolynomialFeatures`, generating a basis that gives the model the flexibility to fit an arbitrarily complex curve through the training observations. The degree was chosen deliberately at the boundary of catastrophic overfitting — complex enough to memorise every data point in a short financial time series, but structurally incapable of capturing the stable growth dynamics that would generalise to unseen quarters.

- **OLS Training on the Full Sample:** Fitted a `LinearRegression` model on the full polynomial feature matrix. Recorded the training MSE as the in-sample benchmark. The resulting near-zero training MSE is not a model quality signal — it is the expected output of a system with more free parameters than the signal requires, interpolating between every observation and calling the interpolation a "forecast."

- **Out-of-Sample Extrapolation — The Hallucination Test:** Generated a prediction for the next quarter by passing the held-out time index through the fitted 7th-degree polynomial. In the extrapolation region — outside the support of the training data — high-degree polynomials diverge catastrophically. The model, having fitted every wiggle in the training data, has no mechanism to produce a forecast that respects the revenue growth trajectory; instead it extrapolates the most recent polynomial curvature, producing a prediction that bears no relationship to any plausible economic scenario.

- **K-Fold Cross-Validation — Quantifying True Operational Error:** Applied K-Fold CV using `cross_val_score` to estimate the model's generalisation error. By repeatedly training on K−1 folds and evaluating on the held-out fold, K-Fold directly measures the model's performance on data it was not fitted to — the only performance metric that matters for a deployed forecasting system. The resulting CV MSE is the operationally relevant error estimate: what the model would actually produce, on average, on a new quarter it has never seen.

- **Train MSE vs CV MSE Decomposition:** Compared the in-sample training MSE against the K-Fold CV MSE to quantify the variance component of the bias-variance decomposition. The magnitude of the Train→CV gap is the direct numerical measurement of how much error the model's variance contributes on top of its irreducible noise floor. In a well-specified model, this gap is small; in an overfit polynomial, it constitutes the dominant component of total prediction error.

---

### Key Findings

**The Training MSE Was Lying**

The 7th-degree polynomial achieved a near-zero training MSE — a result that, absent diagnostic testing, would pass as a high-quality fit under any naive model evaluation protocol. It is not. The model had more degrees of freedom than the quarterly time series had observations, enabling it to pass an interpolating curve through every data point exactly. This is the statistical equivalent of a curve-fitting exercise: the model describes the training data perfectly and predicts nothing.

**Extrapolation Triggered Catastrophic Hallucination**

The next-quarter forecast generated by the overfit polynomial was not merely imprecise — it was economically incoherent. High-degree polynomials are locally constrained by their training data but globally unconstrained in the extrapolation region; outside the training support, the polynomial follows the curvature imposed by its highest-degree term, which has no relationship to NVIDIA's revenue trajectory. The "forecast" was a hallucination in the technical sense: a numerical output produced with full algorithmic confidence that contains no predictive information about the actual outcome.

**K-Fold CV Revealed the True Operational Risk**

The gap between training MSE and K-Fold CV MSE directly quantified the model's variance cost — the error penalty that the model's excessive complexity imposes on unseen data. This gap is not a statistical abstraction; it represents the forecast error that a risk manager, CFO, or algorithmic trading system would incur if this model were deployed in production. K-Fold CV does not improve the model. It reveals what the model actually is — stripping away the optimistic illusion of in-sample performance and replacing it with an estimate of what performance on unobserved quarters would look like.

**The Case for Regularisation**

The CV results establish the precise economic cost of unregularised complexity on this dataset: the dollar-magnitude difference between training MSE and CV MSE is the variance tax. This finding sets the benchmark against which Ridge or Lasso regularisation can be evaluated — not as a theoretical improvement, but as a measurable reduction in operational forecasting risk.

---

### Economic Interpretation

The bias-variance tradeoff is not an academic concept — it is a model risk framework. In a financial forecasting context, variance is not an abstract property of the estimator; it is the dispersion of forecast errors that a downstream decision-maker must absorb. A model with high variance will produce forecasts that are accurate on average across many hypothetical histories but dramatically wrong on any specific future quarter. For a corporate planning team using the forecast to allocate R&D budget, set earnings guidance, or calibrate inventory, "accurate on average across hypothetical draws" is not a useful property. What matters is accuracy on the actual next quarter — which is the quantity K-Fold CV is designed to estimate.

The hallucinated extrapolation is the most economically legible demonstration of this risk. An analyst presenting the 7th-degree polynomial forecast to a decision-maker would be presenting a number produced by a system that has, by construction, no information about the quarter being forecast. The out-of-sample failure is not a corner case or a data quality problem — it is the direct, predictable consequence of deploying a model whose complexity budget was spent on interpolation rather than generalisation.

K-Fold Cross-Validation is the audit mechanism that makes this failure legible before deployment. Any forecasting pipeline that evaluates model quality on training data alone is not doing model selection — it is doing model flattery.
